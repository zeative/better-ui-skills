# Critique against the bar

Use this to review rendered screenshots, your own or as an independent reviewer. The bar is the popular work in design communities for this kind of screen. Be as demanding as a stranger scrolling past: they give a design about two seconds.

Contents: what the bar looks like · how to review · the rubric · finishing pass · the usual gaps and their fixes · brief for an independent reviewer

## What the bar looks like

These are the traits that popular shots in each category share. Hold the work against them.

**Landing pages**
- The hero leads with a large, believable product visual (an app window, a phone, a stack of interface cards) at 50 to 100% of the container width, with three to six small floating detail cards arranged around it. The floating cards overlap its edges cleanly and never hide its content.
- There is atmosphere behind the product: a gradient wash, rings, a faint grid, a glow. Nothing sits on a flat fill.
- Surfaces are finely finished: hairline borders, soft multi-layer shadows, light top edges, inner panels, glassy navigation pills.
- Every section below the hero carries its own designed mini interface or illustration, tiles are full with no dead bands, and sections alternate in tone so a long page has rhythm.
- One confident accent on a calm base, with logos, avatars and chart marks adding small secondary colour.
- Tight, confident type with large headlines, quiet body copy and clearly staged hierarchy.

**Dashboards and admin**
- A full app shell: a sidebar or pill navigation with an icon on every item and a strong active state (often dark or tinted), a top bar with search, notifications and an avatar, and content as distinct cards on a tinted canvas. Never bare text on white.
- Real imagery inside the interface: a distinct product visual in every product row, faces for customers and the user, illustrated colour icons on the KPI tiles. Almost no row is text only.
- Charts treated as hero graphics: smooth area curves with gradient fills, rounded or hatched bars, thick donuts with a centre figure, a highlighted point with a floating tooltip, sparklines inside stat tiles. Three or more chart forms.
- High density, packed into a regular grid with even gutters, and no truncated or colliding text anywhere.
- One saturated accent on a controlled neutral base, with a dark or contrast surface as an anchor and consistently coded status pills.
- Big bold figures with small muted labels.

**Apps and mobile-first tools**
- One confident, ownable idea you could name in a phrase, carried through the whole surface. Not a generic app look, and not a stock gradient.
- Real visual content inside the interface: illustrated cards, dimensional objects or emoji-like glyphs per item, avatars, textured chart bars.
- A neutral base with one or two accents used sparingly; colour encodes meaning instead of decorating every surface.
- Soft layered depth: wide low-opacity shadows, hairlines, a glassy floating tab bar, one dark card set against light ones, the odd overlapping or tilted element.
- Hierarchy by big jumps: a large display figure or greeting against small quiet labels, often with a type choice that has character.
- Dense with small designed detail (day chips, progress dots, tags, mini charts, toggles) on a clear grid with consistent radii. Nothing clips, wraps awkwardly or collides.
- On desktop, a composed multi-column layout, not a phone column in empty space.

## How to review

1. Look at the desktop first screen for two seconds. What did you notice? If the answer is "text", the showpiece is missing or weak.
2. Look at the full desktop page as a thumbnail. Is every section visually distinct, with something to look at? Where does it go flat?
3. Zoom into the showpiece. Count its designed pieces. Does it look like a real product, or like boxes?
4. Check surfaces and depth: can you tell ground, surface and raised apart? Is there a contrast surface?
5. Check the phone screenshots the same way.
6. Score each dimension, write the three most damaging gaps, fix, render again.

## The rubric

Score each 1 to 10. Anything under 8 is unfinished work.

| Dimension | 5: competent but plain | 7: good, a notch below the bar | 9: at the bar |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The one idea** | None: pleasant cards that could be any product in the category | A motif exists but is small or decorative; the hero is a standard card, chart or gradient | One ownable concept from this product's world, large, carrying real content, echoed in the details. You can name it in a phrase |
| **Art direction** | Pleasant, could be any product in the category | A recognisable direction, applied to colours and type but not fully to composition and visuals | A look carried through every element, with nothing left at library defaults |
| **Showpiece** | A headline and buttons, or a simple box standing in for the product | A real product visual, but thin: few details, no lifted-out cards, no atmosphere | A detailed, believable visual with floating details and atmosphere; the first thing you see and worth a second look |
| **Richness** | Mostly text; few or no images, icons, charts or illustrations | Visuals in the first screen, sparse after it; lists without thumbnails or avatars | Something designed to look at in every section; every repeated item carries a small visual; several chart or illustration forms |
| **Depth and finish** | Flat: one shadow or none, content on the bare page | Cards and shadows present but uniform and timid | Clear levels, layered shadows with light edges, a contrast surface, consistent radii, nothing unfinished |
| **Colour** | Neutral with one colour used for everything, or a rainbow of pastels | A decent palette, used evenly over many surfaces | Tightly committed: a neutral base, one dominant colour in large confident fields, one hot accent |
| **Typography** | One size family, weak contrast between levels | Clear hierarchy but timid: one face at mid weights, sizes close together | Dramatic scale contrast: the key figure or line dominates, in a face with character, with small quiet labels |
| **Composition** | Stacked blocks, equal weight, empty areas | Balanced but predictable; same-weight cards; some dead space | One dominant object, a clear second and third, overlap and scale contrast, columns that end together |
| **Phone** | Desktop narrowed; visuals shrunk or dropped | Reflowed sensibly; showpiece weaker | Recomposed for a thumb; still striking |

Also confirm the floor: text is readable everywhere, nothing overflows, controls work. A page that fails the floor is not at the bar whatever it scores.

## Finishing pass

Showcase work has no loose ends. On both the desktop and the phone screenshot, read every string and look at every edge:

- **Clipped text.** No string ends in an ellipsis or is cut by its container. Widen the column, shorten the text, or allow two lines.
- **Collisions and missing spaces.** Labels that run into each other ("ordersToday"), a tag sitting on top of a legend, a caption wrapping to three lines beside a bar.
- **Covered content.** A floating card, stamp or tooltip hiding the names, figures or chart marks beneath it. Overlaps belong on margins and empty corners.
- **Gutters.** The left and right page margins are equal on the phone. Cards do not touch or nearly touch one screen edge.
- **Overlaps.** Stacked avatars and chips do not hide each other's content.
- **Empty bands.** A sidebar with a void in the middle, a column that ends far above its neighbour, a card that is half empty.
- **Legends and labels.** Every chart series has a swatch; every figure has a unit.
- **Repeats.** The same thumbnail, avatar colour or tile used again and again.
- **Mixed languages.** Glossy emoji-style icons inside a flat outlined design, soft shadows beside hard offset ones, a rendered centrepiece beside a default chart. Everything on the screen should look drawn by the same hand.
- **One meaning per colour.** The accent that means "done" must not also mean "today" and "missing".

## The usual gaps and their fixes

- **"There is no idea" or "it reads as a template."** Find the motif in the product's world (SKILL.md step 3), rebuild the hero from it at large scale with real content, echo it in three details, and replace any library-default colours.
- **"Everything has equal weight."** Make one object dominate the first screen, fold minor tiles into it as chips or shrink them, and vary card size and treatment so the eye has a first, second and third stop.
- **"The first screen is mostly text."** Build the product visual from `showpiece.md`: an app window or object with real content, floating cards, atmosphere behind.
- **"The hero is empty" or "it opens on zeros."** Seed the app with sample data in a mid-use state so the showpiece is full and coloured on first load.
- **"The product mock looks like a wireframe."** Triple its density: navigation with icons, figures with deltas, a real chart, rows with avatars and status pills. Use real names and numbers.
- **"It goes flat after the hero."** Give each section its own visual: a bento of feature tiles with mini interfaces, a large demonstration, a stat band, testimonial cards with portraits, pricing cards with a featured plan, a closing band with a visual.
- **"Content sits on the bare page."** Wrap it: an app shell with sidebar and top bar, cards on a tinted ground, a dark or accent feature card.
- **"No imagery."** Thumbnails in lists, illustrated portraits for people, dimensional objects on item cards, an illustration at the heart of the hero module, more than one chart form.
- **"The imagery is generic."** One glyph recoloured for every item, initials for every person, thin line icons everywhere. Draw distinct objects and portraits (`showpiece.md`).
- **"Everything is the same."** Vary tile sizes and treatments; give categories their own hues; alternate section surfaces.
- **"Depth is timid."** Increase ground-to-surface contrast, use the layered shadow scale, add light top edges, lift the featured item.
- **"The colour is monotonous."** Concentrate the accent, add a counter-accent, add one contrast surface.
- **"The colour is a rainbow."** Too many hues at full strength. Keep one dominant accent and one counter-accent; reduce category hues to small marks.
- **"The type is generic."** Use the direction's display face, larger and tighter; add eyebrow pills, emphasised phrases, big figures.
- **"There is dead space."** Enlarge the showpiece, let elements overlap section edges, use the full width on desktop with a multi-column composition, balance column heights, size each tile's visual to fill its tile, tighten the gaps between sections.
- **"The page is monotone."** One tone from top to bottom. Add a section in the opposite tone and a tinted band, raise the contrast between cards and ground, and vary the section recipe.
- **"The phone version lost the magic."** Recompose the showpiece for narrow screens instead of hiding or shrinking it.

## Brief for an independent reviewer

The independent review is the single most effective step in this skill. A builder scores their own work a point or two too high and misses the faults a stranger sees in seconds: a card covering data, a half-empty tile, avatars that are just initials, a palette straight from a template.

Hand the reviewer the screenshots and this brief, and nothing about your intentions or what you already fixed:

> You are a senior visual designer who knows what popular Dribbble shots in this category look like. Review a design built for this brief: "[the user's request, one line]".
>
> Screenshots, all of which you must open with your image-reading tool: [list every file from the audit output: the desktop parts, the phone sheets, the thumbnail].
>
> First read `[absolute path]/references/critique.md`: the section "What the bar looks like" for this category, the rubric, and the finishing pass.
>
> Then return, as plain text: (1) a score from 1 to 10 for each rubric dimension, judged against that bar, and an overall score; an 8 means a Dribbble audience would not notice a quality gap, so do not give it lightly; (2) the six most damaging gaps as specific instructions, most important first: what is wrong, where exactly, and what to do instead; (3) every finishing fault you can see: clipped or colliding text, elements covering content, uneven margins, half-empty cards, misaligned rows, repeated or placeholder-looking imagery.
>
> Be demanding and concrete. Do not praise. Do not suggest changing the product's content or scope; judge the design.

Act on the list in order; where a dimension scored 6 or lower, rework that aspect instead of patching it. Then render again and ask a **fresh** reviewer, not the same one, because a reviewer who has seen the earlier version grades the improvement instead of the result.

Keep going until a fresh reviewer gives 8 or higher overall with no dimension under 7 and lists no finishing faults. Two rounds is a minimum. Most work that reaches the bar takes three or four. Stop after five at the latest, and say in the handoff what the last reviewer scored.
